# Credentialed Arboriculture Knowledge Corpus — Hampton Roads, Virginia (Virginia Beach · Norfolk · Chesapeake · Portsmouth)

*A vetted reference library meeting the strict source filter: only peer-reviewed papers, credentialed authors (PhD / faculty / extension specialist / State Forester / ISA Board Certified Master Arborist), government agencies, university extension services, and works cited by those. Trade blogs, contractor marketing, YouTube, forums, and Wikipedia are excluded from the corpus itself and used here only as non-citable finding aids.*

## TL;DR
- A rigorous, credential-verified corpus is fully achievable for all 11 subject areas: the foundational science (Shigo/Smith CODIT, Gilman pruning, Kane/Slater/Dahle biomechanics, Watson/Bassuk roots/soil, Duryea hurricane ratings, Smiley/Fraedrich risk) is published in peer-reviewed journals, USDA Forest Service outlets, and land-grant extension services — and the large majority is open access.
- The single most valuable open-access repository is ISA's peer-reviewed journal *Arboriculture & Urban Forestry* (and its predecessor *Journal of Arboriculture*); USDA Forest Service Treesearch and Virginia Cooperative Extension / Virginia Department of Forestry supply the government-grade regional material for the Mid-Atlantic coastal plain.
- The science is strongest on pruning cuts, CODIT, and hurricane species ranking, and genuinely uncertain on decay-detection accuracy, strength-loss formulas, and whether structural pruning durably strengthens branch unions — each flagged explicitly so the AI agent never presents contested findings as settled fact.

## Key Findings
Every one of the 11 requested subject areas is covered by credentialed sources, with the single most authoritative/foundational source flagged (★) in each section. Coastal Virginia is well served: Duryea's University of Florida hurricane studies specifically rate **southeastern coastal-plain species** (live oak, loblolly pine, sweetgum, American holly, laurel oak, etc.), and Virginia Tech / Virginia Cooperative Extension / Virginia Department of Forestry cover the exact regional pest-and-disease complex. The chief gaps are not in availability but in *certainty* — several widely-taught quantitative tools (strength-loss formulas, decay-tool accuracy, the ANSI 25% foliage rule) are weaker than practitioners assume, and are documented in Appendix C.

## SECTION 1 — TREE BIOLOGY & DECAY

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Shigo & Marx, *Compartmentalization of Decay in Trees* (USDA Forest Service, 1977).

1. **Compartmentalization of Decay in Trees (CODIT).** Alex L. Shigo (USDA Forest Service forest pathologist) & Harold G. Marx (publications specialist). USDA Forest Service, Agriculture Information Bulletin No. 405, 1977. Stable URL: research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/5292. **Open access.** Establishes the four-wall CODIT model: trees respond to wounding with chemical and physical boundaries (Walls 1–4) that resist the spread of decay; trees do not "heal" but wall off damage and grow over it. The single most influential teaching document in modern arboriculture.

2. **Compartmentalization: a conceptual framework for understanding how trees grow and defend themselves.** Alex L. Shigo. *Annual Review of Phytopathology* 22:189–214, 1984. **Paywalled.** The peer-reviewed scholarly synthesis of the compartmentalization concept.

3. **Compartmentalization today.** Kevin T. Smith, PhD (USDA Forest Service, Northern Research Station, Durham, NH). c. 2006. Stable URL: nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2006/nrs_2006_smith-k_001.pdf; Treesearch record 18529. **Open access.** Updates and clarifies CODIT — critically noting the walls are *conceptual*, not literal "bricks and mortar," and citing the Liese & Dujesiefken (1996) proposal that "D" can denote damage/dysfunction as well as decay.

4. **Whither compartmentalization of decay in trees?** Kevin T. Smith, PhD. *Annals of Botany* 125(5):iv–vi, 2020. DOI: 10.1093/aob/mcaa035. **Open access.** Commentary reaffirming CODIT's origin as a practitioner teaching tool built on 10+ years of forest-pathology research.

5. **Using the CODIT model to explain secondary metabolites of xylem in defence systems of temperate trees against decay fungi.** Morris, Hietala, Jansen, et al. *Annals of Botany*, 2020. DOI: 10.1093/aob/mcaa035 (companion). Modern peer-reviewed refinement linking CODIT to xylem secondary-metabolite biochemistry.

6. **Wounding, Compartmentalization, and Treatment Tradeoffs.** Kevin T. Smith, PhD. *Journal of Arboriculture* 14(9):226, 1988. **Open access** (auf.isa-arbor.com/content/14/9/226). Practitioner-facing overview of wounding effects, relevant to injection/treatment decisions.

*Also foundational (books, paywalled):* Shigo, *A New Tree Biology* (1986) and *Modern Arboriculture* (1991), Shigo & Trees Associates — the standard references for branch collar / branch bark ridge anatomy and wound response.

## SECTION 2 — PRUNING SCIENCE (the research basis behind ANSI A300 Part 1)

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Edward F. Gilman's UF/IFAS pruning research program (hort.ifas.ufl.edu/woody).

1. **UF/IFAS "Landscape Plants" pruning research site** (Mature tree pruning / Thinning / Reducing / Structural pruning pages). Edward F. Gilman, PhD, Professor Emeritus, University of Florida Dept. of Environmental Horticulture. **Open access.** Establishes the practice-critical foliage-removal limits. Verbatim: *"ANSI A300 pruning standards allow up to 25% of live foliage removal on mature trees, but this is too much in many circumstances and not enough in others… There should be a very good reason, such as to reduce likelihood of branch failure, when removing more than ten percent of the live foliage on mature trees."* Up to ~25% may be thinned from young trees; if more than 25–30% must be removed on anything but a young tree, split the job across two or more sessions at least a growing season apart. Defines lion's-tailing / over-thinning as substandard and identifies it as an income-driven malpractice.

2. **Structural Pruning: A Guide for the Green Industry** and associated ISA conference proceedings. Edward F. Gilman, Brian Kempf, Nelda Matheny, Jim Clark. Urban Tree Foundation, 2013. **Open-access proceedings PDF** (isa-arbor.com). Establishes structural pruning's primary objective — developing/maintaining a dominant leader and subordinate branches to prevent codominant-stem failure — as one of the five ANSI A300 pruning objectives.

3. **Reduction-pruning-for-storm-resistance research.** Edward F. Gilman et al., University of Florida (summarized by the NY State Urban Forestry Council). Establishes that reducing large-aspect-ratio branches (large relative to the trunk) and shortening branches markedly reduces branch motion and subsequent storm damage — reframing pruning as a risk-management tool.

*Supporting:* Shigo, *Tree Pruning* (1989) — the origin of the three-cut method, flush-cut vs. collar-cut distinction, and the documented consequences of flush cuts (larger decay columns). Topping is universally documented across these sources to cause decay, weakly attached epicormic sprouts, and stress; it has no legitimate arboricultural application.

## SECTION 3 — TREE BIOMECHANICS & FAILURE

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Dahle, James, Kane, Grabosky & Detter, "A Review of Factors That Affect the Static Load-Bearing Capacity of Urban Trees," *A&UF* 43(3):89–106, 2017 — the field's synthesis review.

1. **A Review of Factors That Affect the Static Load-Bearing Capacity of Urban Trees.** Gregory A. Dahle, PhD, BCMA (West Virginia University); Kenneth R. James (Univ. of Melbourne / ENSPEC); Brian Kane, PhD (UMass Amherst); Jason Grabosky, PhD (Rutgers); Andreas Detter. *Arboriculture & Urban Forestry* 43(3):89–106, 2017. **Open access.** Synthesizes ~30 years of urban-tree biomechanics; load-bearing capacity is proportional to the cube of stem diameter — cross-section size/shape matters more than wood strength.

2. **Tree Biomechanics Literature Review: Dynamics.** Kenneth R. James, Gregory A. Dahle, Jason Grabosky, Brian Kane, et al. *A&UF* 40(1):1–15, 2014. **Open access.** Reviews dynamic sway, damping, and branch-mass effects in open-grown urban trees (distinct dynamics from forest trees).

3. **Failure Mode and Prediction of the Strength of Branch Attachments.** Brian Kane, PhD, et al. **Open access (ResearchGate/A&UF).** Establishes the growing consensus that branch-to-trunk diameter ratio (aspect ratio) predicts attachment strength better than angle of attachment.

4. **Included-bark / natural-bracing research.** Duncan Slater, PhD (Senior Lecturer in Arboriculture, Myerscough College, UK) & A.R. Ennos. Key papers: *"The Level of Occlusion of Included Bark Affects the Strength of Bifurcations in Hazel (Corylus avellana),"* *A&UF* 41(4):194–207, 2015 (**open access**); *"Assessment of the Load-Bearing Capacity of Bark-Included Junctions in Crataegus monogyna in the Presence and Absence of Natural Braces,"* *A&UF* 46(3):210, 2020 (**open access**); Slater et al. 2014 (interlocking-wood-grain "clever clip" model of normal branch junctions). Verbatim finding: bark-included bifurcations' *"modulus of rupture was 24% lower than normally formed bifurcations, while stepwise regression showed that the best predictors of strength… were the diameter ratio and width of the bark inclusion, which explained 16.6% and 8.1% of the variability."* Cup-shaped, partially occluded inclusions were on average **36% stronger** than junctions with included bark at the apex. Establishes that natural bracing higher in the crown is a primary *cause* of included-bark formation.

5. **Tree Biomechanics: A White Paper from the 2010 International Meeting and Research Summit at the Morton Arboretum.** Dahle, Grabosky, Kane, Miesbauer, Peterson, Telewski, Koeser, Watson. *A&UF* 40:309–318, 2014. **Open access.** Consensus research agenda.

6. **Determination of Strain Patterns Across the Root-Stem Transition Zone in Urban Trees.** Beezley, Dahle, Miesbauer, DeVallance. *A&UF* 46:321–332, 2020. **Open access.** Root-plate / root-stem stability.

*Credentials confirmed:* **Brian Kane** — Massachusetts Arborists Association Professor, Dept. of Environmental Conservation, UMass Amherst; PhD; ISA Certified Arborist; 2014 Alex Shigo Award. **Greg Dahle** — Associate Professor, WVU Davis College; PhD (Rutgers); ISA Board Certified Master Arborist; Qualified Tree Risk Assessor. **Duncan Slater** — PhD, Senior Lecturer, Myerscough College.

## SECTION 4 — TREE RISK ASSESSMENT (science underlying ISA TRAQ)

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Smiley & Fraedrich, "Determining Strength Loss from Decay," *Journal of Arboriculture* 18(4):201–204, 1992.

1. **Determining Strength Loss from Decay.** E. Thomas Smiley (ISA BCMA/PhD, Bartlett Tree Research Laboratories) & Bruce R. Fraedrich, PhD (Bartlett Tree Research Laboratories, Charlotte, NC). *Journal of Arboriculture* 18(4):201–204, 1992. DOI: 10.48044/jauf.1992.040. **Open access.** The Bartlett strength-loss formula, tested on Hurricane Hugo–broken and surviving Charlotte oaks (mostly *Quercus alba, Q. falcata, Q. phellos*; 52 of 54 broken oaks had internal decay). Verbatim: *"When the formula was used with a 33% strength loss threshold, it would predict 50% of the failures in 90 mph winds while calling for 'unnecessary' removal of 12% of surviving decayed trees."*

2. **Comparing Formulae that Assess Strength Loss Due to Decay in Trees.** *A&UF* 27(2):78, 2001. **Open access.** Compares the Smiley/Fraedrich formula, Mattheck's t/R ≥ 0.3 buckling rule, and others; documents that Bartlett later advised *against* strict formula use in favor of sound-wood thresholds (Fraedrich 1999).

3. **Non-destructive assessment of internal decay in three hardwood species of northeastern North America using sonic and electrical impedance tomography.** Nicholas J. Brazee, Robert E. Marra, L. Göcke, P. van Wassenaer. *Forestry: An International Journal of Forest Research* 84(1):33–39, 2011. DOI: 10.1093/forestry/cpq040. **Paywalled.** Foundational validation of combined sonic + electrical-resistance tomography.

4. **Assessing the Likelihood of Failure Due to Stem Decay Using Different Assessment Techniques** / **Do advanced assessment techniques change assessors' rating of likelihood of stem failure due to decay?** Okun, Brazee, Cunningham-Minnick, Clark, Burcham, Kane. *Forests* 14(5):1043, 2023. DOI: 10.3390/f14051043. **Open access.** Critical uncertainty finding: advanced tools (resistograph, tomography) did not consistently change — and sometimes *added uncertainty to* — assessors' failure-likelihood ratings.

5. **Impact of assessor on tree risk assessment ratings and prescribed mitigation measures.** Koeser, Smiley, et al. *Urban Forestry & Urban Greening*, 2017. DOI: 10.1016/j.ufug.2017.03.027. **Paywalled.** Documents substantial inter-assessor variability in TRAQ-style ratings.

6. **Risk Assessment and Risk Perception of Trees: A Review of Literature.** *A&UF* 45(1):26, 2019. **Open access.** Comprehensive review underpinning ISA TRAQ.

7. **Foundations of Tree Risk Analysis (t/R).** Bond, with contributions from Christopher J. Luley, PhD, Brian Kane, and Scott Cullen. **Open access PDF.** Establishes and critiques the Mattheck t/R decay threshold.

*Practitioner companion (paywalled, copyrighted):* Smiley, Matheny & Lilly, *Best Management Practices: Tree Risk Assessment* (ISA, 2011) — the BMP paired with ANSI A300 Part 9.

## SECTION 5 — ROOTS, SOIL & PLANTING

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Watson & Himelick, *The Practical Science of Planting Trees* (ISA, 2013).

1. **The Practical Science of Planting Trees.** Gary Watson, PhD (Lead Scientist Emeritus, Morton Arboretum) & E.B. Himelick. ISA, 2013. **Paywalled (book).** The synthesis reference on transplanting, root loss, critical root zone, and establishment.

2. **Transplant shock / root-shoot balance research.** Gary Watson, PhD, Morton Arboretum. Establishes that transplant-shock severity depends on tree size, and that larger trees take proportionally longer to restore a functional root-shoot balance after planting.

3. **Early Flare Root Development on Nursery Field-Grown Seedling Stock.** Gary W. Watson & Angela M. Hewitt (Morton Arboretum). *Forests* 11(10):1031, 2020. DOI: 10.3390/f11101031. **Open access.** Root-system architecture and structural-root-depth research; Watson chairs the Green Industry Working Group on Structural Root Depth.

4. **CU-Structural Soil.** Nina Bassuk, PhD (Professor & Director, Urban Horticulture Institute, Cornell) & Jason Grabosky, PhD (Rutgers). First scientific paper 1995; *CU-Structural Soil: A Comprehensive Guide*, 2015 (hort.cornell.edu/uhi). **Open access.** Establishes the compaction-resistant stone-lattice/soil medium that bears pavement loads while permitting root growth. Grabosky, Haffner & Bassuk, *A&UF* 35(5):271–278, 2009, documents the stone-plus-clay-coating water/soil mechanics.

5. **Ground Penetrating Radar Accurately Locates Tree Roots in Two Soil Media Under Pavement.** Bassuk, Grabosky, Mucciardi, Raffel. *Journal of Arboriculture & Urban Forestry* 37:160–166, 2011. **Open access.** Non-destructive root mapping — relevant to construction/excavation damage assessment.

6. **Root pruning and stability of young willow oak.** E.T. Smiley. *A&UF* 34(2):123–128, 2008. **Open access.** Directly relevant to construction/excavation root damage and critical-root-zone definition. See also Smiley, Key & Greco, "Root barriers and windthrow potential," *J. Arboriculture* 26(4):213–217, 2000.

*Credentials:* **Nina Bassuk** — PhD, Professor, Cornell UHI; 100+ papers; co-author *Trees in the Urban Landscape*. **Gary Watson** — PhD, Morton Arboretum; past ISA President; Richard W. Harris Author's Citation Award.

## SECTION 6 — STORM DAMAGE & WIND RESISTANCE (coastal Virginia priority)

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Duryea, Kampf & Littell, "Hurricanes and the Urban Forest: I. Effects on Southeastern United States Coastal Plain Tree Species," *A&UF* 33(2):83, 2007 — the most directly applicable study for Hampton Roads.

1. **Hurricanes and the Urban Forest: I. Effects on Southeastern United States Coastal Plain Tree Species.** Mary L. Duryea, PhD (University of Florida), Eliana Kampf, Ramon C. Littell. *A&UF* 33(2):83–97, 2007. **Open access.** Combines five 2004–2005 hurricanes with four prior storms. Highest-survival species (directly relevant to VA): **sand live oak, American holly, southern magnolia, live oak, wax myrtle, sweetgum, crapemyrtle, dogwood, and sabal palm.** Laurel oak had significantly poorer survival than live oak and sand live oak — an important local caution given laurel oak's prevalence in Tidewater.

2. **Hurricanes and the Urban Forest: II. Effects on Tropical and Subtropical Tree Species.** Duryea et al. *A&UF* 33(2):98, 2007. **Open access.** Companion; useful for conifer survival data (slash vs. longleaf pine).

3. **Selecting Tropical and Subtropical Tree Species for Wind Resistance** (FOR120/FR175, 2007) and **Wind and Trees: Surveys of Tree Damage in the Florida Panhandle after Hurricanes Erin and Opal** (FR010). Mary L. Duryea & Eliana Kampf, UF/IFAS EDIS. **Open access.** FR010 ranks North Florida coastal-plain species including **loblolly pine (82% still standing after a hurricane)**, sweetbay magnolia (97%), and sycamore (92%).

4. **Expanding a Hurricane Wind Resistance Rating System for Tree Species Using Machine Learning.** Salisbury, Koeser, et al. *A&UF* 51(2):128, 2025. **Open access.** Extends Duryea's 137-species ratings via machine learning; explicitly notes Duryea's original process gave limited description of the rating method and lacked clear category definitions (see Appendix C).

The Duryea 2007 work remains described in the literature as *"the most comprehensive set of wind resistance ratings for urban trees."* Restoration pruning after storm damage and remove-vs-restore decision-making are addressed within the UF/IFAS Urban Forest Hurricane Recovery Program series (Gilman/Duryea, EDIS, open access).

## SECTION 7 — SPECIES-SPECIFIC RESEARCH (Mid-Atlantic / Coastal Virginia)

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Kane, Farrell, Zedaker et al., "Branch Strength of Bradford Pear," *A&UF* 33(4):283, 2007 (Virginia Tech co-authored — regionally apt).

1. **Branch Strength of Bradford Pear (Pyrus calleryana var. 'Bradford').** Brian Kane, PhD, Robert Farrell, Shepard M. Zedaker & D.W. Smith (Virginia Tech). *A&UF* 33(4):283, 2007. **Open access.** First empirical test of Bradford pear branch strength; confirms aspect ratio predicts attachment strength; uniquely, half the branch failures occurred at large laterals, so branch and attachment breaking stresses did not differ (contrary to earlier studies).

2. **Structural Pruning in Callery Pear Does Not Change Apparent Branch Union Strength in Seventh Year Static Load Field Testing.** Richard G. Rathjens, T. Davis Sydnor, Jason Grabosky & Greg Dahle. *A&UF* 47(3):123–130, 2021 (*Pyrus calleryana* 'Redspire'). **Open access.** Key uncertainty flag — verbatim: *"No differences were found in testing branch union strength for either pruned or unpruned trees, suggesting that more time is needed to determine the long-term benefits of structural pruning."*

3. **Branch strength loss implications for silver maple converted from round-over to V-trim.** Dahle, Holt, Chaney, et al. *A&UF*, 2006. **Open access.** Utility line-clearance pruning implications for a common brittle-wooded species.

4. **Examining strain propagation in the lateral branch attachment of white oak (Quercus alba) and chestnut oak.** Eckenrode, Dahle, Smiley, DeVallance, et al. *Urban Forestry & Urban Greening* 73:127591, 2022. DOI: 10.1016/j.ufug.2022.127591. **Paywalled.**

Growth-habit, failure-mode, and wind-resistance data for **live oak, loblolly pine, sweetgum, American holly, southern magnolia, dogwood, crapemyrtle, laurel oak, and pecan** are carried by the Duryea hurricane papers (Section 6). Species-level pruning tolerance and growth habit are documented in Gilman's UF/IFAS "Landscape Plants" species files (open access). Bald cypress, water oak, willow oak, southern red oak, red maple, mulberry, and pecan appear across VCE and USDA FS species/silvics literature (USDA FS *Silvics of North America*, open access).

## SECTION 8 — PESTS & DISEASES OF THE MID-ATLANTIC

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Virginia Department of Forestry Forest Health program and the annual *Virginia Forest Health Highlights* (USDA Forest Service / VDOF).

1. **Emerald Ash Borer: Options for Landowners** (ENTO-76) and **Emerald Ash Borer** (HORT-69). Eric R. Day & Scott Salom (Dept. of Entomology, Virginia Tech); Lori Chamberlin, Katlin Mooneyham, Meredith Bean (VDOF); Jason Fisher & Adam Downing (VCE). Virginia Cooperative Extension. **Open access** (pubs.ext.vt.edu). EAB (*Agrilus planipennis*) is present in all regions of Virginia; insecticidal treatment is viable before ~30% canopy damage; "D"-shaped exit holes and S-shaped galleries are diagnostic.

2. **Virginia Forest Health Highlights** (annual; e.g., 2023). USDA Forest Service Forest Health Protection + Virginia Department of Forestry. **Open access** (fs.usda.gov/foresthealth). Covers southern pine beetle (*Dendroctonus frontalis* — low but persistent given the large loblolly resource; thinning is the most effective preventive), Ips engraver beetles, EAB, hemlock woolly adelgid, spotted lanternfly, and beech leaf disease.

3. **Bacterial Leaf Scorch of Landscape Trees** (VCE 3001-1433). Virginia Cooperative Extension / Virginia Tech, 2024. **Open access.** *Xylella fastidiosa*, vectored by sharpshooter leafhoppers; in Virginia most often on oak (red oak group), elm, and sycamore; distinguished from abiotic marginal scorch by a characteristic yellow band between brown and green leaf tissue; lab confirmation required. (See also the peer-reviewed genome note: "Genome Sequence of a *Xylella fastidiosa* Strain Causing Sycamore Leaf Scorch Disease in Virginia," PMC4153481.)

4. **First Report of Laurel Wilt Disease Caused by Raffaelea lauricola on Sassafras in Virginia.** K.M. DeWitt, L.K. Johnson, L.A. Chamberlin (VDOF); A.H. Kennedy (USDA APHIS); M.A. Hansen & E.A. Bush (Virginia Tech). *Plant Disease*, 2022. DOI: 10.1094/PDIS-11-21-2616-PDN. **Peer-reviewed.** Documents laurel wilt's arrival in Virginia — a live threat to redbay (SE Virginia) and sassafras (statewide).

5. **Laurel Wilt: Current and Potential Impacts and Possibilities for Prevention and Management.** Olatinwo, Fraedrich, Mayfield, et al. *Forests* 12(2):181, 2021. DOI: 10.3390/f12020181. **Open access.** USDA FS Southern Research Station synthesis.

6. **Recovery plan for laurel wilt on redbay and other forest species.** Hughes, Smith, Ploetz, Kendra, Mayfield, Hanula, Hulcr, et al. *Plant Health Progress* 16(4):174–210, 2015. USDA FS. **Open access** (Treesearch 49872).

7. **Biscogniauxia (Hypoxylon) Canker.** Kelly Oten, PhD (Assistant Professor & Extension Specialist, Forest Health, NC State University) & Courtney Johnson. NC State Extension, 2025. **Open access** (content.ces.ncsu.edu/biscogniauxia-hypoxylon-canker). Establishes *Biscogniauxia atropunctata* (formerly *Hypoxylon atropunctatum*) and *B. mediterranea* as latent/opportunistic **stress** pathogens and "one of the major contributing factors of the oak decline disease complex" (red oaks more susceptible than white oaks; no chemical cure — management is stress reduction). Cites primary literature Bassett & Fenn 1984 (*Plant Disease*), Tainter et al. 1983, Freeman et al. 2019 (*Forest Ecology & Management*, DOI 10.1016/j.foreco.2019.117525). USDA-attributed companion: Southern Forest Health (USDA FS + Southern Regional Extension Forestry), "Biology, Ecology, and Management of Biscogniauxia (Hypoxylon) Canker in the Southeastern U.S."

8. **VDOF forest-health pages** for southern pine beetle, spotted lanternfly, oak decline, thousand cankers disease, and laurel wilt (dof.virginia.gov). **Open access, government.**

*Nationally authoritative additions:* USDA Forest Service Forest Insect & Disease Leaflets cover southern pine beetle, oak wilt (*Bretziella fagacearum*), Armillaria root rot, and *Ganoderma*; spotted lanternfly is covered by Penn State Extension and Virginia Tech/VDOF. All are open access and meet the source filter.

## SECTION 9 — TREE HEALTH DIAGNOSIS

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Manion, *Tree Disease Concepts* (Prentice Hall; 1st ed. 1981, 2nd ed. 1991) — the decline-spiral framework.

1. **Tree Disease Concepts (2nd ed.).** Paul D. Manion, PhD (Professor of Forest Pathology, SUNY College of Environmental Science and Forestry). Prentice Hall, 1991 (402 pp.; 1st ed. 1981). **Paywalled (print, out of print).** Introduces the decline-disease *spiral*: **predisposing factors** (long-term — age, genetics, site/soil, climate, pollution — lowering baseline resistance), **inciting factors** (short-term triggers — drought, defoliation, frost), and **contributing factors** (secondary agents — opportunistic cankers such as Biscogniauxia, bark beetles, Armillaria — that carry a declining tree to death). This three-factor model is the backbone of distinguishing biotic from abiotic decline.

2. **Tree Declines: Four Concepts of Causality.** *Journal of Arboriculture* 14(2):29, 1988. **Open access** (auf.isa-arbor.com/content/14/2/29). Peer-reviewed; documents the Sinclair→Manion lineage of decline-causality models (confirms the framework in the peer-reviewed record where the textbook is paywalled).

3. **Diagnosing Plant Damage** (Virginia Master Gardener Handbook, Ch. 6) and **How to Evaluate a Tree** (SPES-313P, referencing Eric Wiseman, PhD, VT urban forestry). Virginia Cooperative Extension / Virginia Tech. **Open access.** Supply the biotic-vs-abiotic decision framework and crown-dieback pattern methodology: whole-canopy or major-portion dieback → suspect roots (gradual = biotic like Armillaria/Verticillium; sudden = abiotic like chemical/freeze/drought); scattered single-branch dieback → suspect canker or borer; always examine the healthy/diseased tissue junction. Establishes that in urban landscapes most tree deaths are **abiotic** (soil, root, chemical, and human-activity causes such as improper pruning, root cutting, and girdling).

## SECTION 10 — URBAN FORESTRY & TREE VALUATION

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCES:** i-Tree (USDA Forest Service, peer-reviewed suite) and the CTLA *Guide for Plant Appraisal, 10th ed.* (2019).

1. **i-Tree.** David J. Nowak, PhD, and the USDA Forest Service (research.fs.usda.gov/products/dataandtools/i-tree). **Open-access, peer-reviewed** software suite (i-Tree Eco, Streets, Landscape) quantifying pollution removal, carbon sequestration, avoided stormwater runoff, and energy effects. Anchor papers: Nowak & Crane (2000); Nowak (2007); Lin, Kroll & Nowak, "An uncertainty framework for i-Tree Eco: a comparative study of 15 cities across the United States," 2021. Nowak candidly notes i-Tree quantifies the monetary value of only four or five benefits and that returns are conservative.

2. **Guide for Plant Appraisal, 10th Edition.** Council of Tree and Landscape Appraisers (CTLA), published by ISA, 2019. **Paywalled (book).** Defines the three appraisal approaches — Cost Approach (Trunk Formula Technique / Cost Compounding Technique), Income Approach, and Sales Comparison Approach — and revises depreciation into three factors: condition (health, structure, form), functional limitations, and external limitations.

3. **Tree Appraisal and the Value of Trees** (FNR-473-W). Lindsey Purcell, ISA Board Certified Master Arborist, urban-forestry specialist, Purdue University. **Open access.** Extension explainer of the CTLA methods.

*Uncertainty flag (see Appendix C):* an independent group of experienced North American plant appraisers publicly critiques the 10th edition (10thguideforplantappraisal.info) for *"ambiguities, contradictions, deficiencies, errors, and significant conceptual and logical flaws,"* with some appraisers continuing to use the 9th edition (CTLA 2000). Practitioner disagreement here is real and should be disclosed by the AI agent.

## SECTION 11 — ARBORICULTURAL SAFETY RESEARCH

**★ FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE:** Ball et al., "A Review of United States Arboricultural Operation Fatal and Nonfatal Incidents (2001–2017)," *A&UF* 46(2):67, 2020.

1. **A Review of United States Arboricultural Operation Fatal and Nonfatal Incidents (2001–2017): Implications for Safety Training.** John Ball, PhD (South Dakota State University) et al. *A&UF* 46(2):67, 2020. **Open access.** "Contact with Objects and Equipment" accounted for 359 fatal (41.5%) and 189 nonfatal (42.8%) incidents; struck-by-falling-branch was 104 fatal (12.0%) / 56 nonfatal (12.7%); details chipper, chainsaw, and struck-by-tree patterns and their training implications.

2. **Work-Related Fatalities Associated With Tree Care Operations — United States, 1992–2007.** Dorothy A. Castillo & Cammie K. Menéndez (NIOSH). *MMWR (Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report)* 58(15):389–393, CDC/NIOSH, 2009. **Open access, government.** Of 1,285 tree-worker fatalities, 44% occurred while pruning/trimming and **34% involved a fall** — the definitive national fatality dataset.

3. **NIOSH / CDC FACE (Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation) arborist investigations.** CDC/NIOSH (stacks.cdc.gov). **Open access, government.** Case-level struck-by and fall fatality investigations with specific prevention recommendations (work plans, job briefings, minimum distances of 1.5–2× tree height for uninvolved workers).

4. **Virginia 16VAC25-73, "Regulation Applicable to Tree Trimming Operations."** Virginia Safety and Health Codes Board / Virginia Department of Labor and Industry (VOSH). Statutory authority § 40.1-22 of the Code of Virginia; **effective April 27, 2011**; based on ANSI Z133.1-2006. **Open access** (law.lis.virginia.gov/admincodefull/title16/agency25/chapter73/). Scope (16VAC25-73-10): arboriculture safety requirements for pruning, repairing, maintaining, and removing trees, cutting brush, and using equipment. Explicitly **excludes** line-clearance tree trimming (→ 16VAC25-90-1910.269) and logging (→ 16VAC25-90-1910.266). **This is the free, citable government equivalent to the paywalled ANSI Z133.**

5. **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.269** (Electric Power Generation, Transmission, and Distribution; Subpart R). U.S. Dept. of Labor / OSHA. **Open access** (ecfr.gov; osha.gov). Paragraph (r) explicitly covers line-clearance tree trimming near energized conductors — the governing federal standard for arborists working near power lines (minimum approach distances, qualified line-clearance arborist requirements).

6. **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.266** (Logging Operations; Subpart R). U.S. Dept. of Labor / OSHA. **Open access.** Governs felling, limbing, bucking, and chipping where the objective is land clearing / timber harvest; per 16VAC25-73, land-clearing tree removal defaults to this standard.

*Do NOT ingest or reproduce ANSI Z133 or ANSI A300 text* — both are copyrighted, paywalled standards. Reference them and rely on the Virginia regulation and the two OSHA standards for free, citable safety content.

## APPENDIX C — WHERE THE SCIENCE IS THIN OR CONTESTED (flag these in the app)

1. **Decay-detection accuracy.** Sonic tomography and the resistograph are validated for detecting/quantifying decay, but published accuracy varies with tree cross-sectional geometry; default tomography software settings can materially over- or under-estimate decay extent, and advanced tools do **not reliably improve — and can add uncertainty to —** assessors' failure-likelihood ratings (Okun, Brazee, Kane et al., *Forests*, 2023). The AI agent should treat tomograms/resistograph traces as decision aids, not verdicts.
2. **Strength-loss formulas.** The Smiley/Fraedrich 33% threshold and Mattheck's t/R ≥ 0.3 rule are widely taught but imperfect predictors (the 33% threshold missed roughly half of failures in the original hurricane data), and Bartlett itself moved away from strict formula use toward sound-wood thresholds. Kane's early career work showed the formulas omit important variables. Do not present any single formula as authoritative.
3. **Structural-pruning durability.** Callery pear ('Redspire') structural pruning showed **no measurable branch-union strength gain after seven years** (Rathjens, Sydnor, Grabosky & Dahle, 2021). The long-term structural benefit of young-tree pruning is sound arboricultural theory but not yet robustly demonstrated experimentally for all species.
4. **Percentage foliage-removal limits.** The ANSI A300 "up to 25%" figure is a practice generalization, not a species-specific experimental threshold; Gilman explicitly calls it *"too much in many circumstances and not enough in others"* and advises staying under ~10% on mature trees without a specific defect-reduction reason.
5. **Plant appraisal.** Active, public practitioner disagreement over the *Guide for Plant Appraisal* 10th edition's methods and internal consistency; some qualified appraisers still use the 9th edition. Any appraisal feature should disclose this and default to the locally-endorsed ISA-chapter species-rating guide.
6. **Wind-resistance ratings.** Duryea's ratings are observational and expert-opinion based; category definitions were not precisely specified and many common species remain unrated (being addressed by machine-learning extensions, Salisbury et al., 2025). Treat species rankings as strong guidance, not precise probabilities.
7. **CODIT literalism.** The four "walls" are conceptual boundaries, not fixed physical structures; treating them as literal "bricks and mortar" is a documented misinterpretation (K.T. Smith).

## Recommendations
1. **Build the core from open-access ISA journals + USDA Forest Service + Virginia extension first.** This yields the highest-credibility, lowest-cost coverage and satisfies the strict source filter with minimal paywall friction. Prioritize, in order: *Arboriculture & Urban Forestry* full-text, USDA FS Treesearch, and Virginia Cooperative Extension / VDOF.
2. **Make the Duryea coastal-plain hurricane papers and the Virginia Tech/VDOF pest publications the regional backbone.** These are the most directly load-bearing sources for Hampton Roads storm and pest decisions.
3. **Purchase only the essential paywalled books and standards for internal reference** — *Guide for Plant Appraisal* 10th ed., Watson & Himelick, Manion, and the ANSI A300/Z133 standards. **Never ingest ANSI or copyrighted book text into the AI corpus;** encode only the underlying facts from free equivalents (Virginia 16VAC25-73, OSHA 1910.269/.266) and paraphrased summaries.
4. **Tag every corpus entry with an uncertainty flag where Appendix C applies,** so the AI agent presents contested tools (strength-loss formulas, t/R, decay-tool accuracy, structural-pruning benefit, the 25% rule, appraisal methods) with appropriate hedging rather than as settled fact.
5. **Record provenance metadata on every entry** — author + credential, institution, journal/publisher, year, DOI/stable URL, and open-access status — exactly as structured above, so the agent can cite and the user can audit.
6. **Benchmarks that would change these recommendations:** (a) If the app must output *quantitative* failure probabilities, do not deploy any single strength-loss formula until a species-appropriate validation study supports it — await or commission validation. (b) If appraisal features are added, monitor CTLA's forthcoming 11th edition (in development following the 10th-edition public comment period) and migrate when it is published and generally accepted. (c) If the agent begins advising near-power-line work, escalate the safety layer to the full OSHA 1910.269(r) requirements and require a qualified line-clearance arborist — general arboriculture guidance is legally insufficient there.

## Caveats
- Some author credentials/affiliations were confirmed via institutional pages and ResearchGate rather than the primary article PDF; verify each author's exact title at the point of citation before publication.
- A handful of DOIs and exact page numbers were captured from search results and secondary listings and should be re-verified against the publisher record of record before the corpus goes live (notably Section 3 branch-attachment paper, Section 7 Eckenrode DOI, and Manion edition/year).
- *Tree Disease Concepts* and *The Practical Science of Planting Trees* are print books; cite the concepts and corroborating peer-reviewed papers (e.g., *J. Arboriculture* 14(2):29 for the decline concept) rather than reproducing text.
- ANSI A300 and ANSI Z133 are copyrighted; this corpus references and points to them but must not reproduce their text — a legal, not merely editorial, constraint.
- This report deliberately excludes the many trade-blog and contractor sources that surfaced during research (e.g., tree-service company blog posts on CODIT and Bradford pear); they corroborated the credentialed findings but do not meet the source filter and were not admitted to the corpus.
